# Off-hours rental stalls at property-level gates

No US company has built a dominant, verifiably profitable business renting limited-hours space to third parties while the owner is closed, and the most visible attempt, Peerspace, filed Chapter 11 on **August 30, 2026** ([WhatNow](https://whatnow.com/news/trending/san-francisco-venue-rental-marketplace-with-45000-spaces-files-for-chapter-11-bankruptcy/)). The binding constraints sit with the building rather than the booking: certificates of occupancy, place-of-assembly permits, lease use clauses, alcohol and food-permit rules, and premises liability for injuries on property the platform does not own. Demand arrives episodically. Peerspace claims **more than 1 million** bookings and **45,000+** venues over about ten years ([Peerspace](https://www.peerspace.com/blog/peerspace-announces-kevin-yuann-as-ceo)), which works out to roughly two bookings per venue a year by my arithmetic, while its host fee runs **15–20%** ([Peerspace fees](https://www.peerspace.com/legal/terms/fees-overview)). The relative successes avoided that trap by selling a standard, high-frequency unit such as parking reservations or hotel day passes, or by selling software to institutions that remain the legal operators of their buildings, as Facilitron does for school districts. Peerspace's filing is best explained as litigation-driven: a guest paralyzed in a 2024 fall was set for trial on **August 31**, the day after the petition, and Peerspace was the only defendant still in that case ([Bondoro](https://app.bondoro.com/preview/cases/Peerspace_Inc/case-summary)). Aggregator data show no secured debt, which argues against a lender-driven collapse, but no court record or company filing was read.

## Property rules, not software, set the binding limits

Each space type meets a different binding constraint, and the sharpest ones live in the building's legal status rather than in the booking software. The table summarizes the pattern across the space types in the question.

| Space type | Binding constraint | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Restaurants, cafés, kitchens | Operator cost and capital | Spacious closed December 31, 2019 as WeWork cut costs ([Restaurant Dive](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/wework-shutters-restaurant-coworking-startup-spacious/569018/)); Pilotworks shut overnight in 2018 after failing to raise capital ([Restaurant Dive](https://restaurantdive.com/news/pilotworks-shuts-down-overnight/539798)) |
| Offices and meeting rooms | Fixed leases meeting a demand shock | Breather closed more than 400 sites in December 2020 ([Commercial Observer](https://commercialobserver.com/2020/12/breather-flex-space-closing-covid/)) |
| Retail storefronts | Landlord terms and foot traffic | b8ta ended US operations after failing to reach a deal with landlords ([Modern Retail](https://www.modernretail.co/retailers/b8ta-shutters-u-s-operations-after-failing-to-reach-a-deal-with-landlords/)) |
| Event venues and studios | Premises liability, permits, absorption | Peerspace in Chapter 11; EventUp folded into Tripleseat ([FSR](https://www.fsrmagazine.com/industry-news/tripleseat-acquired-vista-equity-partners-merge-gather/)); no scale or failure data found for gym or studio platforms |
| Schools | Direct-cost fee caps, custodial labor | California's Civic Center Act limits fees to direct costs ([Facilities Dive](https://www.facilitiesdive.com/news/california-bill-would-expand-school-districts-cost-recovery-options-for-fa/748352/)); Conroe ISD rentals ran about $150,000 short ([Houston Chronicle](https://www.chron.com/news/houston-texas/education/article/conroe-isd-tutoring-spaces-rental-texas-21257490.php)) |
| Churches | Certificates of insurance, tax rules | One Pittsburgh church requires renters' certificates to name it as named insured ([FPCP](https://fpcp.org/facility-rental)); rent from real property is generally excluded from unrelated business income ([UUA](https://www.uua.org/node/103782)) |
| Parking | Few constraints, high frequency | SpotHero passed $1 billion in parking reservations sold ([SpotHero](https://spothero.com/press/spothero-eclipses-1-billion-in-parking-reservations-sold)) |
| Hotel amenities | Supply already licensed to the operator | ResortPass signed a global day-use deal with Marriott on May 27, 2026 ([Skift](https://skift.com/2026/05/27/marriott-signs-resortpass-deal-why-hotels-are-pushing-to-sell-more-than-rooms/)) |
| Residential pools and homes | Local ordinances and public-pool rules | Minnesota enforcement against Swimply hosts ([Minnesota Lawyer](https://minnlawyer.com/2024/12/16/pool-owners-allege-shadow-rulemaking/)) |

A certificate of occupancy fixes a building's legal use and capacity, and event use can require separate approvals. New York requires a Place of Assembly Certificate of Operation from the Department of Buildings, plus a separate FDNY permit with an annual inspection by default ([NYC DOB](https://home4.nyc.gov/site/buildings/dob/project-categories-paco.page)). In Los Angeles, a banquet hall seeking alcohol sales, live entertainment, dancing and hours to 2 a.m. went through a discretionary conditional use permit ([LA City Planning](https://planning.lacity.gov/dcpapi2/meetings/document/80513)). These are per-property approvals with conditions attached to the site, so a per-booking workflow cannot clear them. The sources cover only New York and Los Angeles in depth. Chicago, San Francisco and Texas zoning were not researched.

Alcohol is the sharpest constraint in California. A one-day license cannot go to a for-profit business or an individual organizer, who need a caterer holding a Type 58 permit instead ([SF.gov](https://www.sf.gov/serve-alcohol-at-your-outdoor-special-event)). A registered 501(c)(3) can apply directly ([Palo Alto ABC FAQ](https://www.paloalto.gov/files/0bc183fc-6092-464c-a011-62f756665212/ABC-FAQ.pdf)), so a commercial platform cannot put its own licensing behind a booking. Food follows the same logic. San Francisco requires a pop-up's host to hold its own health permit, renewed yearly, and caps operations at three days a week ([sf.gov](https://sf.gov/node/10266)). In Alameda County, an inspector halted a 2018 pop-up dinner mid-service, and the department held that only the licensed operator at an address could run a food business there ([SF Chronicle](https://preview-prod.w.sfchronicle.com/food/restaurants/article/Alameda-County-declares-pop-up-restaurants-illegal-13175453.php)).

Leases add a second gate. Use clauses can restrict business type and hours, and a sublease generally requires landlord consent, with the sub-lessor still bound by the master lease ([NDV Law](https://ndvlaw.com/can-you-sublease-commercial-property-without-consent/)). Tax adds friction. The Form 1099-K threshold reverted to $20,000 and 200 transactions ([IRS](https://irs.gov/newsroom/irs-issues-faqs-on-form-1099-k-threshold-under-the-one-big-beautiful-bill-dollar-limit-reverts-to-20000)), and whether hotel occupancy taxes reach event rentals turns on local definitions of "space" ([City of Waco](https://www.waco-texas.com/Departments/Budget-Finance/Hotel-Occupancy-Taxes)).

Public owners face a price ceiling. According to a Facilities Dive summary, California districts are again limited to direct costs, because a 2012 expansion that counted a share of operating costs has lapsed ([Facilities Dive](https://www.facilitiesdive.com/news/california-bill-would-expand-school-districts-cost-recovery-options-for-fa/748352/)). A single aggregator reports that Wyoming capped district fees at the hourly cost of staff, equipment and utilities from July 1, 2026 ([Citizen Portal](https://citizenportal.ai/articles/8086585/wyoming/legislative/committees/select-committees-task-force/select-water-committee/new-law-caps-school-facility-rental-fees-at-actual-costs-starting-july-1-2026)). In Shakopee, Minnesota, rentals grew every year since 2021, but custodial staffing became the named pressure point ([Citizen Portal](https://citizenportal.ai/articles/6363600/Minnesota/School-Boards/SHAKOPEE-PUBLIC-SCHOOL-DISTRICT/District-facilities-rentals-bring-rising-revenue-but-raise-staffing-and-scheduling-challenges)). Under cost-based pricing, custodial labor becomes the binding cost of every after-hours booking.

## Thin liability cover meets a 15–20% fee

Platforms cover premises risk thinly. Peerspace provides up to **$1 million** of supplemental general liability per booking and a **$25,000** property-damage guarantee for US venues, which its terms say does not constitute an insurance policy. Hosts and guests must carry primary cover ([Peerspace help center](https://intercom.help/peerspace-108cf19aff8e/en/articles/10119116); [Peerspace guarantee terms](https://www.peerspace.com/legal/terms/property-damage-guarantee)). Courts have not settled platform liability for violence on hosted property. A San Francisco judge let a negligence claim over a fatal house-party shooting proceed against Airbnb ([KQED](https://www.kqed.org/news/12001736/case-against-airbnb-in-fatal-house-party-shooting-can-proceed-judge-rules)). A federal court dismissed claims over a shooting at an Airbnb rental, on claim-element grounds rather than Section 230, according to one expert's summary ([Goldman](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2022/11/snap-and-airbnb-arent-liable-for-tragic-shooting-jackson-v-airbnb.htm)). No appellate Section 230 ruling for an event-space platform surfaced in this research.

Residential supply draws the heaviest rules. Swimply hosts in Minnesota challenged state health-department enforcement as an invalid unpromulgated rule ([Minnesota Lawyer](https://minnlawyer.com/2024/12/16/pool-owners-allege-shadow-rulemaking/)), and Rockland County, New York, sought the platform's host and transaction records from June 2018 onward ([Rockland County Business Journal](https://rcbizjournal.com/2025/01/05/rockland-county-goes-after-swimplys-records-to-crack-down-on-backyard-pool-rentals/)). San Jose's code did not allow private pool rentals as of 2022 ([Patch](https://patch.com/california/campbell/why-renting-your-pool-illegal-san-jose)).

Fees are the second squeeze. Splacer kept 15% of each booking in its early years ([FarWest Capital](https://www.farwestcapital.com/blog/a-better-place-for-offsites/)). Peerspace charged hosts 15% and renters 5% in 2017 ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/business/peerspace-raises-11-million-to-provide-original-event-locations)), and its current host fee is 15–20% plus a guest fee. Owners accept such fees when the platform brings buyers they could not reach. SpotHero's partner garages agreed to give about 15% of parking fees because the network brought incremental parkers ([Harvard D3](https://d3.harvard.edu/platform-digit/submission/spothero-kicks-parking-frustrations-to-the-curb/index.html)). Owners without that proof push back. Glendale Unified board members called Facilitron's commission "cost prohibitive" for community groups, and one said, "I see no need to give that commission away" ([Crescenta Valley Weekly](https://www.crescentavalleyweekly.com/?p=153294)).

Demand compounds the fee problem. Codi reported that 35% of its customers leased an office fewer than five days a week ([Bisnow](https://www.bisnow.com/news/national/coworking/san-fran-flex-office-startup-is-like-the-timeshare-for-office-116007)), and Croissant's co-founder said spaces opened and closed "several times a month" ([Technical.ly](https://technical.ly/2017/12/05/is-the-coworking-boom-over/)). Peerspace's own totals imply an average booking of about **$500** ($500 million in claimed sales over 1 million bookings), so a 15–20% host fee yields roughly $75 to $100 per booking before guest fees. That arithmetic is mine, built on company claims. Vendor studies also report organic click-through declines of up to 61% on informational queries where Google shows AI Overviews ([Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/google-ai-overviews-drive-61-drop-organic-paid-ctr-464212)), but none measured venue or parking marketplaces.

## Fixed-cost operators failed while marketplaces got absorbed

Operators that carried fixed costs failed under shock. Breather had spent nearly all of its roughly **$122 million** in venture funding by late 2020 ([BetaKit](https://betakit.com/breathers-us-uk-subsidiaries-reportedly-file-for-insolvency/)). It faced a landlord suit seeking **$79,000** in unpaid rent after terminating a SoHo lease ([Allwork](https://allwork.space/2020/12/breather-sued-by-new-york-city-landlord/)), closed more than 400 sites in December 2020 ([Commercial Observer](https://commercialobserver.com/2020/12/breather-flex-space-closing-covid/)), and sold key assets of its listings platform to Industrious in 2021 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breather_(company))). One trade report put the sale at a **US$3 million** cash price, a single-source figure ([The Logic](https://thelogic.co/?p=135399)). REEF Technology's ghost-kitchen business cut **750** staff in May 2022, with its parking division exempt ([Commercial Observer](https://commercialobserver.com/2022/05/softbank-backed-reef-technology-laying-off-750-employees/)). It then closed three New York facilities, with a WARN notice citing 53 jobs ([Bisnow](https://www.bisnow.com/new-york/news/industrial/ghost-kitchen-reef-technology-closing-three-new-york-locations-125368)), and shifted to licensing its technology ([Restaurant Business](https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/technology/reef-closes-more-ghost-kitchens-it-shifts-focus-tech)). Kitchen United closed all its Kroger-based units in November 2023 and pivoted to software ([Supermarket News](https://www.supermarketnews.com/independents-regional-grocers/kitchen-united-closes-all-kroger-locations-as-company-shifts-to-software-focus)). Retail followed the same path. Showfields filed Chapter 11 in 2023, blaming lingering pandemic effects and rigid leases ([Retail TouchPoints](https://www.retailtouchpoints.com/news/showfields-files-for-bankruptcy-protection-as-it-seeks-to-reorganize/137806/)), and Neighborhood Goods closed all four stores by January 2024 ([Modern Retail](https://www.modernretail.co/operations/as-neighborhood-goods-shuts-down-brands-await-final-payments/)).

Marketplaces got absorbed rather than scaled. EventUp, a Chicago event-venue marketplace, went to Gather around 2018 and then into Tripleseat after Vista's 2020 merger ([FSR](https://www.fsrmagazine.com/industry-news/tripleseat-acquired-vista-equity-partners-merge-gather/)). VenueBook was acquired by restaurant-rewards platform Seated in August 2020 ([Restaurant Dive](https://restaurantdive.com/news/seated-acquires-events-booking-platform-venuebook/583750)). In both cases the buyer was a software or restaurant platform, and the marketplace became a feature of a venue workflow product. Splacer's New York listings grew from about 236 in late 2015 to about 700 by March 2017 ([FarWest Capital](https://www.farwestcapital.com/blog/a-better-place-for-offsites/); [DNAinfo](https://www.dnainfo.com/new-york/20170301/williamsburg/splacer-venues-events-rental)). Aggregators now list it as having ceased operating in June 2025, and no press coverage of the closure surfaced ([StartupIM](https://startupim.com/company/splacer)).

Four pressures explain the pattern in this record. Fixed-cost supply turns a demand shock into insolvency. Per-property gates keep listings unbookable or expensive to bring online. Episodic demand keeps per-venue frequency low. Owners resist fees on demand they can source themselves, which squeezes the margin a platform needs for sales and insurance. Sarah Tavel's marketplace framework, as a secondhand summary reports it, argues that a platform must first win one constrained market ([Tavel](https://sarahtavel.medium.com/the-hierarchy-of-marketplaces-introduction-and-level-1-983995aa218e)). The space platforms here spread across meetings, offices, events and shoots at once. The record also contains almost no verified unit economics. Most scale figures are company claims, so "no winner" describes what can be verified more than what has been ruled out.

## Winners sold standard units, licensed supply or software

The durable results share one feature: they separate the booking from the building's legal and operating burden. SpotHero sells parking reservations, a standard, high-frequency product. It passed **$1 billion** in reservations sold by October 2022 ([SpotHero](https://spothero.com/press/spothero-eclipses-1-billion-in-parking-reservations-sold)) and raised a **$50 million** Series D in 2019 ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/startups/2019/digital-parking-company-spothero-raises-50-million-in-series-d-funding-round/)). ResortPass sells day passes to hotel pools and spas. The hotels already hold the licenses and supply the space, pay a subscription, and ResortPass takes a cut of each booking ([Hotel Technology News](https://hoteltechnologynews.com/2022/12/resortpass-lands-26-million-to-expand-its-hotel-pool-and-amenities-access-platform/)). It raised **$26 million** in November 2022 ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20221130005386/en/ResortPass-Raises-26-Million-Series-B-for-Hotel-Amenity-Access)), and on May 27, 2026, Marriott signed a global agreement for ResortPass to sell day-use access at participating hotels ([Skift](https://skift.com/2026/05/27/marriott-signs-resortpass-deal-why-hotels-are-pushing-to-sell-more-than-rooms/)). Bounce, which books luggage storage in partner shops and hotels, raised a **$19 million** Series B in November 2024 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/11/12/bounce-grabs-19m-to-expand-its-traveller-convenience-network/)).

Facilitron is the closest analog to off-hours space at scale, because school gyms, fields and rooms sit idle after the school day. Its own release says it "surpassed $500 million in revenue generated for U.S. public schools" ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/facilitron-helps-public-schools-generate-over-half-a-billion-dollars-in-hidden-revenue-302539321.html)). A single third-party profile says that figure is cumulative gross transaction volume, not company revenue, and that the platform takes 6–12% of external rentals ([checkthat.ai](https://checkthat.ai/brands/facilitron)). The vendor also claims more than 10,000 US K-12 schools and colleges use the platform ([Facilities Dive](https://www.facilitiesdive.com/news/facilitron-school-rental-platform-facilities-management/717477/)). Its district portals say it handles payments and certificates of insurance ([Santa Clara USD](https://www.facilitron.com/scusd95051)). Districts stay the legal operators: they keep the buildings, the calendar and the rules, while the platform sits in front of them. That structure is my inference from the portals and board records, not a stated contract term. The model carries real friction. A Virginia school division's Facilitron contract takes 10% of revenue ([Soar](https://cpo.soar.com/articles/7970704/Virginia/School-board-approves-new-facility-rental-fee-schedule-over-nonprofit-concerns-101)), and the fee lands on renters, which drew the Glendale objection. Facilitron still earns per booking, but venue software sold by subscription, such as Tripleseat, does not depend on booking volume, which is one reason venue software outlasted venue marketplaces in this record.

Asset-light operators that sold software kept going. LiquidSpace reportedly keeps about 10% of each booking ([Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/us/how-this-company-is-helping-businesses-make-the-most-of-excess-office-space.print)). Its 2025 products target enterprise buyers, including a federal workplace-booking pilot selected in March 2025 ([LiquidSpace](https://resources.liquidspace.com/press-release-gsa-fedworkplace)), though the company reports those results itself. KettleSpace, a New York flexible-workspace operator, sold its physical business to WorkChew in April 2022 and kept its technology ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20220426005505/en/)). The pattern is consistent: sell the software or the booking, and leave the lease and the labor to someone else.

Caveats limit these lessons. Survival is not profitability, and no survivor's financials surfaced. Facilitron's $500 million and Peerspace's $500 million are both company claims. These winners sell a parking spot, a day pass or a district's booking workflow. None shows that a privately owned storefront or a restaurant dining room can carry off-hours rentals at scale.

## Peerspace's filing followed one injury suit, not a visible collapse

Peerspace filed a voluntary Subchapter V petition on Sunday, August 30, 2026, in the Northern District of California, case 3:26-bk-30766, before Judge Dennis Montali ([Inforuptcy](https://www.inforuptcy.com/browse-filings/california-northern-bankruptcy-court/3:26-bk-30766/bankruptcy-case-peerspace-inc)). The company says the filing aims to resolve pending litigation and does not reflect the health of its platform, and it expects no effect on listings, bookings or payouts ([WhatNow](https://whatnow.com/news/trending/san-francisco-venue-rental-marketplace-with-45000-spaces-files-for-chapter-11-bankruptcy/)). The platform stayed open. Aggregator summaries report about 83 remote employees, no physical offices, and roughly 12,500 accepted but unexecuted bookings worth about $11.6 million at the petition date ([Konkurseridag](https://us.konkurseridag.com/bankruptcy/peerspace-inc/canb-26-30766)). Peerspace launched its All Inclusive Venues service on September 2, three days after the filing ([Webull](https://www.webull.com/news/15508430142956544)).

The litigation trigger is a single case. A guest was paralyzed in a July 2024 fall down a staircase at a Chicago residence listed on the platform. The guest sued Peerspace and the space's owners and operators in Cook County in September 2024 for negligence and premises liability. Every other defendant settled, good-faith settlement talks with Peerspace failed, and trial was set for August 31, 2026 ([Bondoro](https://app.bondoro.com/preview/cases/Peerspace_Inc/case-summary)). A Chapter 11 petition triggers the automatic stay, which halts that trial. The same summary reports that Peerspace planned to propose a plan resolving the claim if talks failed. A jobs-board page says the trial began on the filing day, but that claim could not be corroborated ([Simplify](https://simplify.jobs/c/Peerspace)).

The balance sheet fits a litigation-driven filing. Subchapter V is available only to small business debtors under a debt ceiling, which was **$3,424,000** when Peerspace filed ([ABI](https://www.abi.org/member-resources/blogs?page=40)). As a matter of statute, that ceiling counts only noncontingent, liquidated debts (11 U.S.C. §101(51D); the statute text was not retrieved for this research). Aggregators report no secured debt, small trade debt and mostly disputed or unliquidated litigation claims ([Bondoro](https://app.bondoro.com/preview/cases/Peerspace_Inc/case-summary)). Total liabilities are listed at $10 million to $50 million by one aggregator ([Konkurseridag](https://us.konkurseridag.com/bankruptcy/peerspace-inc/canb-26-30766)) or $16.8 million by another ([LeadIQ](https://leadiq.com/c/peerspace/5a1d89d62400002400633220)). Either range fits one large contingent claim dominating the liabilities. The record contains no lender-driven distress, and no primary source reports revenue or liquidity figures.

Revenue evidence is thin and conflicting. Dealroom models revenue at $10.0 million in 2020, $4.9 million in 2021 and $14.7 million in 2022 ([Dealroom](https://app.dealroom.co/companies/peerspace)), and trackers put cumulative funding between about $33.5 million and $40 million, with no disclosed equity round since 2018 ([CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/company/peerspace/financials)). Peerspace's own 2020 post said pandemic closures had increased unauthorized parties on the platform, which prompted a Social Events Policy ([Peerspace](https://www.peerspace.com/blog/social-events-policy)). That is a documented risk, not a documented cause of this filing. No source states the damages sought in the Cook County case, so whether the claim exceeds the $1 million per-booking cover, or Peerspace's own balance sheet, is unknown. The decisive records are the Cook County docket, the first-day declarations and schedules in the bankruptcy case, the claims deadline of November 9, 2026 and the Subchapter V plan due November 30, 2026 ([Inforuptcy](https://www.inforuptcy.com/browse-filings/california-northern-bankruptcy-court/3:26-bk-30766/bankruptcy-case-peerspace-inc)).

## Conclusion

The pattern across these cases is a mismatch between what a marketplace sells and where the risk sits. A per-booking platform sells a booking, but the costs that decide survival sit in the building (permits, leases, insurance) and in the operation (staff, cleaning, alcohol, food). Models that moved those costs onto a party already licensed to carry them, such as hotels, parking operators and school districts that remain the legal operators, or that sold a unit with recurring demand, survived or grew. Peerspace shows the exposure of the platform layer itself. By an aggregator's account of its petition, it owns, leases or controls none of the listed spaces, yet a premises suit named it, and the petition landed one day before trial. A fee model that does not price the tail of premises liability carries that tail on its own balance sheet.

Two developments would change this reading. A settlement or verdict estimate in the Cook County case would confirm or reshape the litigation account, and a sale process, dismissal or plan that fails to resolve the claim would change the story. Until a primary docket is read, the filing is a documented litigation event rather than a documented model failure. The research also found no verified unit economics for any US space marketplace, so "no winner" describes what can be seen, not what has been proven. All findings rest on search snippets and aggregator summaries. No primary page was read in full, and PACER and the Cook County docket were not accessible.
